# The Atlantic Covenant World

## Canonical Timeline and Continuity Guide

**Version:** 0.1  
**Divergence point:** 1 March 1476  
**Current review horizon:** 1526  
**Outline horizon:** late 1930s  

This file is the working canon for an alternate history in which Portugal and the supporters of Joanna of Castile win a decisive victory at the Battle of Toro. The result is not an instant Portuguese annexation of Castile, but a durable dynastic union that redirects European state formation, Atlantic exploration, religious change, colonialism, slavery, and revolutionary politics.

The timeline is deliberately divided into two levels of certainty:

- **Reviewed canon** has survived a dedicated fifty-year plausibility review and should not be changed casually.
- **Provisional canon** records the intended long-term story but remains open to revision when its fifty-year period receives a full review.
- **Open question** marks a decision that has not yet been made.

Dates followed by `c.` are approximate. Titles used by later historians may differ from those used by contemporaries.

---

## 1. Canonical premises

1. The Battle of Toro ends in an unmistakable Portuguese–Juanist victory. Ferdinand of Aragon is captured, seriously wounded, or otherwise removed from effective command, allowing the military result to produce a political cascade in Castile.
2. Joanna of Castile retains the Castilian crown. Afonso V of Portugal is recognized as her husband and co-ruler, but Castile is not simply annexed to Portugal.
3. A surviving child of Afonso and Joanna, provisionally named **Henrique of Aviz-Trastámara**, supplies the heir needed to make the union durable.
4. Portugal retains control of the dynasty’s most developed naval and Atlantic institutions. Castile remains the larger and more populous kingdom. This imbalance becomes a permanent constitutional tension.
5. Aragon remains separate. Consequently, no historical Spain forms, and the Habsburgs never inherit the combined Spanish monarchy.
6. Columbus does not receive Isabella’s sponsorship in 1492. Northern Atlantic exploration grows instead from English Bristol voyages and later French, Breton, Dutch, and German participation.
7. Portugal still reaches India and encounters Brazil around 1500. Brazil is known and claimed, but it is not rapidly transformed into a large settler or plantation colony.
8. American epidemic exposure is slower, regional, and intermittent. Indigenous societies suffer severely, but most retain enough political continuity to recover, adapt, bargain, and selectively acquire foreign technologies.
9. The European Reformation begins, but repeated continental wars prevent Protestant powers from consolidating as they did in actual history. Protestantism survives in pockets and is carried across the Atlantic by successive refugee waves.
10. In the Americas, refugee Christianity is translated, disputed, printed, and merged with diverse Indigenous political and spiritual traditions. The result is the **American New Reformation**, later called the **Covenant tradition**.
11. The Portuguese-led monarchy eventually develops a southern Atlantic monopoly and a large plantation-slave system in Brazil and the Caribbean. Indigenous polities and northern colonies become important sanctuaries for fugitives.
12. Revolutionary thought emerges from an Atlantic argument about covenant, sanctuary, slavery, accountable rule, and plural sovereignty. It culminates first in a Portuguese-centered revolution, then in a delayed and more destructive English civil war.
13. The Portuguese imperial system declines during the nineteenth century and collapses during the Great Atlantic War of the early twentieth century.
14. A subsequent North American Civil War destroys a short-lived imperial-nationalist project and produces a partially unified Covenant North America.

## 2. Terminology and naming conventions

### The Iberian union

Use **the Western Crowns** as the neutral working name for the dynastic complex after 1476. Other context-specific names are acceptable:

- **Crowns of Portugal and Castile:** formal and constitutional usage.
- **Aviz-Trastámara Monarchy:** dynastic or academic usage.
- **Monarchy of the Ocean:** ideological language favored by the court in its imperial phase.
- **Portuguese monarchy:** common foreign shorthand, often resented in Castile.

Do not call the state “Spain” unless quoting an outsider using the geographical term. Do not treat it as a centralized nation-state in the fifteenth or sixteenth century.

### Religious movements

- **First Wave:** European Protestant refugees and printers cross the Atlantic, chiefly during the middle and later sixteenth century.
- **Second Wave:** Indigenous and creole thinkers transform refugee Protestant ideas into distinct American theologies and political philosophies during the seventeenth century.
- **Third Wave:** revolutionary Covenant thought returns across the Atlantic in the eighteenth century and helps ignite revolutions in Portugal and England.
- **Fourth Wave:** anti-imperial and renewal movements after the Great Atlantic War, ultimately central to the North American Civil War.

“Covenant” is an umbrella term, not a single church. It includes mutually hostile denominations and political traditions.

### Peoples and polities

Avoid treating Indigenous America as a single civilization or unified bloc. Name particular nations, confederacies, cities, leagues, and language communities whenever they have been established in canon. Later reviews must determine which historical peoples survive, migrate, amalgamate, or form new states.

## 3. Master chronology

### 1476–1526 — Formation of the Western Crowns

**Status: reviewed canon.**

A decisive Portuguese–Juanist victory at Toro preserves Joanna’s Castilian claim. Portugal and Castile enter a dynastic union under Afonso V and Joanna. Their son Henrique later joins the crowns without abolishing their separate laws and institutions. Granada falls later than in actual history; there is no 1492 expulsion on the historical model. Portuguese voyages reach India and Brazil, while English and French mariners slowly establish northern Atlantic routes. By 1526 the Americas have contact zones but no European territorial empire comparable to historical Spanish America.

### 1526–1576 — Wars of consolidation

**Status: provisional canon.**

- The Reformation remains vigorous in German, Swiss, Low Country, Scandinavian, and English pockets, but it lacks the historical anti-Habsburg coalition structure.
- France, Austria, Portugal, Aragon, and shifting German princes intervene repeatedly in succession disputes inside the Holy Roman Empire.
- The **First Iberian Coalition War** occurs provisionally in the 1520s or 1530s. Its exact cause, belligerents, and dates require review.
- A first substantial wave of Protestant refugees, printers, translators, artisans, and dissident clergy reaches northern America.
- One or more ambitious Caribbean expeditions are destroyed by hurricanes, disease, supply failure, and local resistance. Their destruction creates a durable cultural fear of the southern passages but does not alone explain the delay in colonization.
- A **Second War of Castilian Succession**, provisionally c. 1548–1557, ends in another victory for the Western Crowns. Aragon is subordinated, absorbed, or bound into the monarchy; the exact settlement remains open.
- A treaty provisionally called the **Treaty of Bordeaux** recognizes or tolerates an Iberian claim to regulate navigation south of a designated Atlantic line. The name, date, and legal terms require review.

**Long-term payoff:** military victory, treaty law, convoy capacity, port access, charts, and intimidation combine with genuine environmental danger to keep rival settlement concentrated in the north.

### 1576–1626 — The northern contact world

**Status: provisional canon.**

- English, French, Dutch, Breton, and some German interests maintain fisheries, seasonal stations, trading factories, missions, and small settlement attempts along the North Atlantic coast.
- Most European settlements survive by charter or treaty under Indigenous sovereignty rather than through immediate conquest.
- Repeated epidemics remain disastrous but geographically staggered. Political institutions survive in many regions, enabling demographic recovery and organized quarantine, migration, diplomacy, and adoption.
- Firearms, metalworking techniques, sailing knowledge, alphabetic print, and European livestock spread unevenly. American crops and medical knowledge move east in return.
- Protestant refugee communities become linguistically and culturally dependent on host nations. Imported confessional disputes begin to change under Indigenous scrutiny.
- The Western Crowns concentrate on Africa, the Indian Ocean, and enforcing their southern maritime claims rather than undertaking a rapid conquest of mainland America.

### 1626–1676 — The Second Wave

**Status: provisional canon.**

- Indigenous printers, translators, jurists, clan leaders, diplomats, and theologians become the principal authors of a distinct American Christianity.
- New doctrines emphasize accountable leadership, covenant as reciprocal obligation, council government, the moral limits of property claims, sanctuary, and the coexistence of overlapping sovereignties.
- These ideas draw differently on the traditions of particular Indigenous societies; they are not merely European Protestantism in Indigenous dress.
- Small English and French colonies remain coastal and politically constrained. Dutch and German commercial enclaves add competing sources of weapons, credit, print, and diplomatic recognition.
- England reaches a durable **Stuart Settlement** rather than experiencing the historical Civil Wars. The crown bargains with a narrower Protestant opposition and preserves more royal power.
- Portuguese settlement in Brazil begins to accelerate, especially where sugar cultivation can be protected by naval power.

### 1676–1726 — Plantation expansion and sanctuary conflict

**Status: provisional canon.**

- The Western Crowns expand plantation zones in Brazil and then into selected Caribbean islands.
- The transatlantic slave trade grows later than in actual history, but expansion may be especially intense once capital, sugar land, shipping, and monopoly protection finally align.
- Enslaved Africans, maroon communities, Indigenous states, dissident ports, and northern traders create overlapping escape and smuggling networks.
- Some Indigenous powers formally recognize a right of sanctuary. Iberian authorities describe this as theft of property and interference with navigation; Covenant writers frame it as a limit on unjust jurisdiction.
- A series of **Caribbean Navigation Wars**, provisionally beginning near the end of the seventeenth century, pits the Western Crowns against changing English, French, Dutch, colonial, and Indigenous coalitions.
- The wars concern convoy inspection, neutral shipping, escaped slaves, privateering, treaty ports, and the legal status of people who cross jurisdictional boundaries.

### 1726–1776 — The Atlantic argument

**Status: provisional canon.**

- The **Third Wave** forms as American Covenant texts circulate through England, France, the Low Countries, German cities, and Iberia.
- European natural-rights philosophy and American traditions of reciprocal sovereignty alter one another. The central revolutionary question becomes not simply whether rulers possess consent, but whether authority remains valid after violating covenant obligations.
- Historical thinkers such as Locke, Montesquieu, and Rousseau may still exist, but their biographies, patrons, targets, and major works differ. Their functions should be redistributed across a wider Atlantic network rather than copied unchanged.
- Abolitionist reasoning develops earlier as a constitutional and jurisdictional argument, though economic emancipation remains fiercely resisted.
- The Western Crowns become overextended: naval commitments, plantation defense, war debt, court rivalry, and unequal representation aggravate Portuguese–Castilian–Aragonese tensions.
- Small northern European colonies begin demanding more autonomy, usually in alliance with or dependence upon neighboring Indigenous powers.

### 1776–1826 — The revolutionary age

**Status: provisional canon.**

- Revolution begins within the overextended Portuguese-led imperial system, provisionally during the 1780s.
- The Portuguese Revolution combines a fiscal crisis, colonial revolt, abolitionist and sanctuary politics, provincial constitutionalism, and conflict over the unequal Western Crowns.
- A counter-revolutionary military commander rises in a role analogous to Napoleon: defending part of the Revolution, centralizing it, conquering neighboring territories, and eventually exhausting the state. Whether this figure is Portuguese, Castilian, colonial, or of mixed Atlantic origin remains open.
- The final settlement produces some form of **Iberian Federation**, constitutional monarchy, or republican federation rather than restoring the old Monarchy of the Ocean intact.
- England’s Stuart order initially survives the Portuguese upheaval. Third-Wave preachers, veterans, printers, abolitionists, and colonial agitators then carry the crisis into Britain.
- The delayed **English Civil War**, provisionally c. 1790–1830, is longer and more destructive than the historical seventeenth-century conflict. It combines religious, parliamentary, colonial, class, and national struggles.
- People analogous in social role to figures of the historical American Revolution agitate simultaneously for autonomy in England’s small colonies and constitutional transformation in the metropole.
- A **Commonwealth of the Isles** or comparable post-Stuart settlement emerges. Its final borders and treatment of Ireland, Scotland, and Wales remain open.

### 1826–1876 — The Atlantic Spring and industrial multipolarity

**Status: provisional canon.**

- A second revolutionary sequence, the **Atlantic Spring**, occurs provisionally between the 1830s and 1850s.
- Revolts concern abolition, land, federal representation, confessional equality, labor, and national self-rule. They affect Europe and the Americas rather than forming a purely European “1848.”
- Several Indigenous-majority or genuinely multinational American great powers industrialize without first being replaced by European settler states.
- Railways and telegraphs strengthen large continental federations but also sharpen disputes between centralized nationalism and layered Covenant sovereignty.
- The former Western Crowns retain large possessions and commercial privileges in Africa, Brazil, Atlantic islands, and parts of the Caribbean, but their system increasingly resembles a brittle multinational empire.
- No unified Germany emerges on the historical Prussian model. France, Austria, Iberia, England, and shifting German federations remain the principal European balance-of-power actors.
- Because Europe lacks the wars of Danish, Austro-Prussian, and Franco-Prussian unification, land doctrine and heavy field artillery develop less coherently than naval gunnery.

### 1876–1926 — Imperial rivalry and the Great Atlantic War

**Status: provisional canon.**

- Centralized nationalist factions in Brazil and other American great powers argue that industrial states must possess overseas bases, protected markets, and African influence.
- The declining Iberian imperial system becomes the object of debt diplomacy, concession hunting, local revolt, and great-power rivalry, paralleling some functions of the historical Ottoman Empire.
- A **South Atlantic War**, provisionally c. 1890–1905, allows Brazil or a Brazilian-led coalition to defeat Iberian forces in a conflict centered on Angola, shipping, or concessions. It foreshadows modern naval war without persuading European armies to abandon older operational assumptions.
- Successive partition crises draw France, England, Austria, German states, American powers, African states, Caribbean leagues, and Iberia into opposed blocs.
- The **Great Atlantic War**, provisionally c. 1918–1927, begins with an Angola or shipping-rights crisis and expands in stages.
- Early campaigning in Europe resembles an enlarged eighteenth-century war of maneuver: professional armies seek decisive flanking battles in the tradition of Blenheim, Leuthen, or Austerlitz.
- Machine guns, mass rifles, rail mobilization, field entrenchment, and eventually new heavy artillery destroy that model. Naval gunnery begins the war more advanced than land artillery.
- Prototype heavy guns—developed by firms or arsenals analogous to Krupp—appear around 1922, just as maneuver gives way to fortified fronts.
- A major fleet campaign near Nova Scotia combines a Jutland-scale contest for sea control with a Guadalcanal-like sequence of confused night actions, convoy battles, island fighting, and an attempted Iberian marine landing.
- Iberia’s imperial network collapses. A settlement provisionally called the **Congress of Dakar** ends the war around 1927 and redistributes bases, mandates, commercial rights, and sovereign claims.

### 1926–c. 1940 — The Fourth Wave and North American Civil War

**Status: provisional canon.**

- Postwar veterans, workers, clergy, Indigenous constitutionalists, anti-imperial organizers, maroons, and colonial subjects generate the **Fourth Wave** of Covenant thought.
- North American politics fracture between a centralized **National Union**, which embraces industrial nationalism and limited overseas empire, and a **Covenant Coalition**, which defends plural sovereignty, regional autonomy, sanctuary, and anti-imperial reconstruction.
- Generals trained in the Great Atlantic War initially rely on immense artillery parks and fixed fortifications.
- North America’s distances, weak transport chokepoints, and dispersed political centers reward radio coordination, mechanized logistics, armored columns, air reconnaissance, and deep operational movement. Tank warfare develops rapidly and may move just beyond the technical level of historical 1945.
- In the final **Covenant campaign**, mobile columns sever the National Union’s east–west routes and split its armies into isolated theaters.
- The capture of the Pittsburgh/Forks corridor functions as a reversed Vicksburg: it breaks the Union’s continental communications.
- A destructive advance across Pennsylvania to the Atlantic functions as an analogue to Sherman’s March, attacking transport, arsenals, administration, and the political credibility of the Union.
- General **Adrian Kessel** is isolated in Ohio or Pennsylvania and surrenders at **Concord House** in a deliberately restrained scene analogous to Appomattox Court House.
- The victors create the **Covenant of North American Nations**, a continental federation or confederation based on layered sovereignty.
- A smaller **Laurentian Union** survives as the remnant of the centralized nationalist and imperial experiment.

## 4. Reviewed canon: 1476–1526

### 4.1 The divergence at Toro, 1476

In actual history, Toro was militarily ambiguous but politically useful to Isabella and Ferdinand. In this timeline the Portuguese–Juanist victory must be impossible to reinterpret.

On 1 March 1476, Prince John’s wing performs at least as well as it did historically. The decisive change occurs elsewhere: Afonso V’s center does not dissolve, Juanist Castilian nobles maintain cohesion, and Ferdinand’s route of retreat is cut. Ferdinand is captured, gravely wounded, or forced from political leadership. Portuguese forces retain the field, prisoners, artillery, and enough enemy standards to make rival claims of victory implausible.

The victory matters only because it triggers a second event: Castilian nobles who had been waiting to see which claimant could protect their interests defect to Joanna. Town councils open negotiations; Isabella’s coalition loses access to revenue and recruits; Aragon cannot rescue Ferdinand without accepting dangerous concessions.

**Canonical result:** by 1477 a negotiated settlement recognizes Joanna as Queen of Castile and Afonso V as husband and co-ruler. Isabella renounces the crown or enters closely supervised retirement. Ferdinand is eventually released under treaty. Neither is executed, avoiding an unnecessarily total dynastic purge.

### 4.2 The dynastic solution, 1477–1495

Joanna and Afonso have a surviving son in 1477 or 1478, provisionally **Henrique of Aviz-Trastámara**. His existence is the largest single biological contingency after Toro and is indispensable to the durable union.

Afonso V dies in 1481. His son John II inherits Portugal. Joanna continues to rule Castile, and Henrique becomes the shared dynastic future of both kingdoms. During this regency-like period the crowns remain legally distinct but build limited common machinery:

- a joint Atlantic council;
- coordinated diplomacy;
- customs and convoy agreements;
- royal postal routes;
- shared naval purchasing;
- arbitration procedures for disputes between Portuguese and Castilian merchants.

The court circulates among Lisbon, Valladolid, and Toledo. Lisbon is the maritime and naval capital, but Castile retains its own treasury, councils, laws, coinage, estates, and political identity.

In 1491, John II’s legitimate son Afonso dies in the historical riding accident. Henrique therefore becomes the strongest legitimate heir to Portugal rather than Manuel of Beja or John’s illegitimate son Jorge.

John II dies in 1495. Henrique, about seventeen or eighteen, succeeds in Portugal and is already Joanna’s heir and probable co-ruler in Castile. The personal union becomes durable without becoming administratively unitary.

**Permanent fault line:** Castile possesses more people, land, and tax capacity; Portugal controls the ruling dynasty’s naval expertise, African possessions, Atlantic charts, and many of its common institutions. Every later constitutional crisis must engage with this imbalance.

### 4.3 Granada and religious policy

The conquest of Granada is delayed, not abandoned. Joanna needs the legitimacy of a Catholic victory, Castilian nobles want land and patronage, and Portugal already possesses a crusading tradition in Morocco. However, the new monarchy must first pacify Castile and decide whether money belongs in Granada, Morocco, or the Atlantic.

**Working dates:** 1486–1496. Castilian armies bear most of the land burden; Portuguese revenues, ships, engineers, and artillery contribute. The long campaign deepens Castilian resentment that the maritime kingdom can influence continental priorities.

There is no exact equivalent of the Spanish Inquisition established under Isabella and Ferdinand in 1478. Anti-Jewish violence, pressure on converts, and demands for religious uniformity remain powerful. They should not be erased. But there is no canonical 1492 mass expulsion, and Manuel’s historical 1496 Portuguese expulsion—closely connected to his proposed marriage into the Spanish royal family—does not occur in the same form.

Larger Jewish and converso populations therefore remain in Iberia. They become disproportionately important in medicine, finance, translation, navigation, printing, diplomacy, and connections with North Africa and the Ottoman world. A centralized royal tribunal may still appear later during war or rebellion, but its creation must have its own alternate cause.

**Long-term thread:** the survival of these communities complicates the monarchy’s later claim to be the champion of Catholic order and supplies people, texts, capital, and exile networks to the revolutionary Atlantic.

### 4.4 The Atlantic without Columbus

Columbus presents his project to Portugal and is rejected. There is no Isabella-centered court prepared to fund him in 1492. He may seek support from Aragon, France, England, or Italian financiers, but no patron immediately accepts the full proposal.

Northern exploration nevertheless has an independent foundation. Bristol merchants had already shown interest in westward voyages during the 1480s. In this timeline:

- exploratory voyages continue through the 1480s and 1490s;
- reports of western land become credible between roughly 1498 and 1505;
- English mariners improve maps of Newfoundland and nearby coasts during c. 1505–1512;
- Breton and French fleets follow;
- by 1526, seasonal fisheries and limited coastal trade are established.

There is no immediate northern conquest. Europeans lack the manpower, fiscal commitment, local intelligence, and nearby island base that aided Spain in the Caribbean. Contact is commercial, diplomatic, predatory, or violent according to local circumstances, but Europeans remain vulnerable guests in most places.

Portuguese navigation still makes an encounter with Brazil around 1500 highly likely. The India route’s Atlantic wind system naturally carries fleets westward, and a Cabral-like expedition remains plausible. Brazil is therefore **known, claimed, mapped in outline, and used for limited trade**, especially dyewood. It is not yet the center of a major colony.

The Caribbean is incompletely mapped. A failed southern expedition may occur around 1520–1525 and become the first source of the later hurricane taboo. The loss must combine storm, poor provisioning, disease, navigational uncertainty, and local resistance. Hurricanes alone cannot keep rival powers away for a century and a half.

### 4.5 Africa and the Indian Ocean

The Portuguese maritime project is already too mature to vanish because of Toro. Bartolomeu Dias rounds the Cape in 1488 or on a similar schedule; Vasco da Gama reaches India in 1497–1498; a Portuguese fleet takes or establishes Goa around 1510 and Malacca around 1511.

The union can provide more money, ships, and recruits, but its internal consolidation and the Granada war prevent implausibly rapid expansion. The eastern empire remains a chain of forts, ports, tribute arrangements, naval patrols, and local alliances—not a vast territorial conquest.

The enlarged monarchy’s African trade may increase enslavement in Iberia and Atlantic islands. However, without a large American plantation complex, there is no sixteenth-century transatlantic slave trade on the historical scale.

### 4.6 European balance of power

Ferdinand becomes king of Aragon in 1479, but he does not possess Castile. Aragon remains an independent Mediterranean power, wounded by Toro and anxious about encirclement.

The Italian Wars still begin after 1494. France initially has greater freedom because Aragon cannot draw upon Castilian resources. The Western Crowns intervene cautiously, using ships, money, diplomacy, and limited forces rather than immediately assuming the historical Spanish role in Italy.

The Habsburgs remain primarily Austrian and Burgundian. There is no Charles V ruling Spain, the Netherlands, Austria, much of Italy, and an American empire. France is less encircled, and the durable European contest becomes a shifting game among France, Austria, the Western Crowns, Aragon, England, and German princes.

The Reformation still begins in 1517. Indeed, the absence of Charles V may make its first decade easier. By 1526 reform movements are growing in German cities and principalities, Switzerland, the Low Countries, Scandinavia, and England.

**Canonical correction:** Protestantism has not yet been reduced to isolated pockets in 1526. That is a later result of repeated wars, failed coalitions, dynastic intervention, and the destruction or exhaustion of its political patrons. Only small numbers of religious emigrants cross the Atlantic in the 1520s; the first substantial refugee wave belongs to the following period.

### 4.7 The Americas in 1526

By the end of the reviewed period:

- English and French mariners maintain seasonal northern routes.
- A few small stations or settlement attempts have failed; others exist only by local permission.
- Northeastern Indigenous nations possess experience with trade, bargaining, hostage exchange, interpreters, new materials, and European factional rivalry.
- Brazil is known and lightly exploited but not densely colonized.
- The Caribbean is imperfectly explored and associated with unusual danger.
- No European power has conquered Mexico.
- There is no Caribbean bridgehead comparable to historical Hispaniola or Cuba.
- Europeans have not reached or conquered the Andes.
- Old World livestock and plants have spread only locally.
- Epidemics occur, but exposure is regional and discontinuous rather than a near-simultaneous hemispheric catastrophe.

The demographic advantage is **institutional survival**, not rapid biological immunity. Some communities still lose horrifying proportions of their people. The crucial difference is that many states, councils, kin networks, ceremonial systems, and military coalitions endure long enough to learn, reorganize, relocate, and recover before the next major shock.

### 4.8 Reverberations beyond the core regions

- **Ottoman Empire:** the conquest of the Mamluk Sultanate in 1516–1517 proceeds broadly as in actual history. Portugal is a stronger Indian Ocean rival, but there is no Spanish-Habsburg superstate contesting the Ottomans across every theater.
- **France and the Ottomans:** France has less need for the exact historical Franco-Ottoman alliance because it is not encircled by Charles V. Cooperation remains possible but develops differently.
- **Safavid Iran:** the Safavid state still emerges around 1501 and may bargain with Portugal against Ottoman or maritime rivals.
- **Mughal India:** Babur’s victory at Panipat in 1526 remains plausible. Portuguese power affects coastal trade more than the politics of the northern interior.
- **West Africa:** Songhai remains strong. Coastal trade expands, including enslaved people, but the absence of a vast American plantation market changes its scale and geography.
- **Southeast and East Asia:** Portuguese seizure of Malacca and contact with China remain plausible. Castilian participation exists under Portuguese direction rather than through a separate Spanish Philippines.
- **World economy:** without early Mexican and Andean silver, Europe’s Price Revolution is smaller, later, or differently sourced. Iberian rulers have less extraordinary credit, Mediterranean and overland commerce remain relatively more important, and the silver flows that historically transformed Ming trade do not develop on the same schedule.

### 4.10 Plausibility audit

**Strong elements**

- A decisive battlefield result can alter the Castilian succession if accompanied by Ferdinand’s capture or incapacitation and rapid noble defections.
- A dynastic union is more credible than immediate Portuguese annexation.
- Portuguese voyages to India and Brazil have causes independent of Columbus and should continue.
- Northern Atlantic exploration can develop from Bristol and fishing interests without a Spanish Caribbean breakthrough.
- No Habsburg Spain follows naturally from Joanna’s defeat of Isabella.
- Delayed conquest allows more Indigenous political institutions to survive successive epidemics.

**Elements requiring discipline**

- Portugal cannot permanently dominate Castile by prestige alone. The constitutional struggle between Portuguese institutions and Castilian demographic weight must remain active.
- Henrique’s birth and survival are necessary contingencies and should be treated as such, not as an inevitable outcome.
- A later Catholic geopolitical offensive cannot retroactively erase the Reformation’s real strength in the 1520s.
- Hurricanes may reinforce avoidance, but only military exclusion, port control, convoy law, secrecy, cost, and repeated failure can sustain a southern monopoly.
- “Technological equivalence” in the Americas should be sector-specific. Some states may match European firearms, fortification, printing, shipbuilding, medicine, or administration while differing profoundly in industrial base and military doctrine.
- Epidemics cannot be made mild. The alternate result comes from spacing, regional variation, institutional continuity, deliberate adaptation, and demographic recovery.

**Overall verdict:** the first fifty years form a credible high-contingency divergence. The least secure point is not the Battle of Toro itself but the union’s ability to survive the deaths and successions of the following two decades. Henrique and a deliberately limited constitutional compact solve most of that problem while creating useful later conflicts.

## 6. Continuity constraints

Future additions should observe these rules unless this document is deliberately revised:

1. Do not introduce an early Spanish-style conquest by another power under a different flag. The setting depends on the absence of a wealthy Caribbean launchpad and on Indigenous political continuity.
2. Do not make all Indigenous societies equally successful. Outcomes must vary by geography, demography, disease exposure, political organization, trade position, and relations with neighbors.
3. Do not make European states passive. Their restraint in the south must arise from cost, war, monopoly enforcement, intelligence limits, and remembered catastrophe.
4. Do not make the Western Crowns a modern centralized state too early. Its strength comes from coordinating distinct crowns; its weakness comes from failing to reconcile them.
5. Do not let the Reformation simply “fail” in 1517. Its political containment and American transformation are processes lasting generations.
6. Do not treat American New Protestantism as a European gift. Indigenous authorship, categories, languages, and institutions become constitutive, especially in the Second Wave.
7. Do not equate conversion with cultural replacement. Syncretism, strategic adoption, translation disputes, and dual religious belonging should be common.
8. Do not use disease as a precise adjustable weapon. Epidemic timing can change; pathogens remain destructive and often unpredictable.
9. Do not assume a delayed slave trade is a small slave trade. Its later concentration may make the seventeenth- and eighteenth-century struggle especially violent.
10. Do not copy historical philosophers, generals, or revolutions beat for beat. Preserve analogous pressures and social roles while allowing different lives, texts, coalitions, and outcomes.
11. Keep naval technology and institutions ahead of European land-war doctrine before the Great Atlantic War, but provide concrete industrial reasons for the lag in heavy artillery.
12. The Covenant victory in North America is not effortless moral progress. It follows civil war, coercion, internal faction, destruction, and contested compromises.

## 7. Open decisions register

These questions should be settled during later fifty-year reviews:

1. What is Henrique’s regnal name in Portugal and Castile, and does Joanna remain active after 1495?

## 8. Fifty-year review template

Each future period should be reviewed under the same headings:

### [YEAR]–[YEAR]

**Status:** draft / reviewed canon  
**Period thesis:** one paragraph explaining the main transformation.

#### A. Chronology

List the principal events in causal order, including relevant successions, wars, treaties, voyages, epidemics, migrations, religious disputes, and economic changes.

#### B. Iberia

Review dynastic politics, crown relations, law, taxation, religion, maritime policy, and imperial administration.

#### C. Europe

Review France, England and the British Isles, Austria and the Habsburgs, Aragon if still separate, Italy, the German states, Scandinavia, the Low Countries, Russia, and the Ottoman frontier as relevant.

#### D. Americas

Review particular Indigenous polities, European settlements, diplomacy, disease, demography, trade, technology, religion, ecology, and territorial control.

#### E. Wider world

Review Africa, the Ottoman and Safavid worlds, South Asia, Southeast Asia, East Asia, and global trade. Include only consequences with a plausible causal route from the divergence.

#### F. Comparison with actual history

Use a compact table separating unchanged background forces from genuine divergences.

#### G. Threads planted and paid off

Record new continuity obligations and identify which older threads now produce consequences.

#### H. Plausibility audit

For every major divergence, ask:

- What is the causal mechanism?
- Who benefits and who resists?
- What resources make it possible?
- What prevents the historical outcome?
- Does the change occur too quickly or too uniformly?
- What unintended consequences should appear?

#### I. Canon decisions and open questions

End with a short list distinguishing newly fixed canon from unresolved choices.

## 10. Revision protocol

- Increment the document version when reviewed canon changes.
- Record changes in version control rather than maintaining an in-file prose changelog.
- A later review may revise provisional dates freely.
- A reviewed event should be revised only when new causal analysis exposes a contradiction or a better solution.
- When revising a foundational event, search the “Long-term threads” table and update every dependent payoff.
- Prefer ranges and uncertainty where the exact year has not been earned by the causal work.
