# Toro Divergence: Decision Ledger

This file preserves *why* the project adopted its major choices. The event record remains in `CANONICAL_TIMELINE.md`.

## Status

- **Canonical:** settled unless a later review reveals a contradiction.
- **Provisional:** the present working answer, open to revision during its fifty-year review.
- **Candidate:** worth preserving, but not yet adopted.
- **Rejected:** considered and deliberately set aside.

When a decision changes, update this file and every dependent passage in the canonical timeline.

## Foundational divergence

### D-001 — Toro must be politically decisive

**Status:** Canonical  
**Period:** 1476

Prince John retains his historical battlefield success, while Afonso V's center avoids collapse. Ferdinand is captured, gravely wounded, or otherwise removed from effective command. Portuguese–Juanist possession of the field, prisoners, artillery, and standards makes competing claims of victory implausible. Castilian nobles then defect to Joanna.

**Reasoning:** A merely clearer tactical victory would not necessarily reverse the historical political result. Ferdinand's removal and the subsequent political cascade are essential.

### D-002 — Joanna rules Castile; Castile is not annexed

**Status:** Canonical

Joanna is recognized as Queen of Castile, with Afonso V as husband and co-ruler. Portugal and Castile enter a dynastic compact while retaining separate laws, estates, treasuries, privileges, and identities.

**Rejected:** An immediate Portuguese conquest or unitary Iberian state.

### D-003 — Henrique makes the union durable

**Status:** Canonical

Afonso and Joanna have a surviving son, provisionally Henrique of Aviz-Trastámara. After the historical death of John II's legitimate son Afonso in 1491, Henrique becomes the strongest legitimate heir to both crowns.

**Reasoning:** A shared heir is necessary and is explicitly treated as a major biological contingency.

**Open:** His final name, regnal numbers, marriage, character, and relationship with Joanna.

### D-004 — The union's central contradiction is permanent

**Status:** Canonical

Castile supplies population, territory, and taxes; Portugal controls the dynasty's strongest naval, African, and Atlantic institutions. Neither can comfortably absorb the other.

**Payoff:** Officeholding disputes, wars of consolidation, revolution, federal experiments, and imperial collapse.

## Iberia, religion, and Europe

### D-005 — Granada falls later, not never

**Status:** Canonical in principle; dates provisional

The Western Crowns eventually conquer Granada, provisionally during 1486–1496. Joanna needs legitimacy, nobles seek patronage, and Portugal has crusading interests, but pacification and competing maritime commitments delay the war.

**Rejected:** Permanent Granadan survival without an additional strong cause.

### D-006 — No identical expulsions in 1492 and 1496

**Status:** Canonical

The exact historical Inquisition and mass expulsions do not occur on their historical schedules. Anti-Jewish violence, coerced conversion, and demands for orthodoxy remain powerful.

**Payoff:** Larger Jewish and converso communities influence medicine, finance, navigation, printing, diplomacy, and later exile and revolutionary networks.

### D-007 — Aragon initially remains independent

**Status:** Canonical through 1526

Ferdinand inherits Aragon but not Castile. Aragon remains a wounded, independent Mediterranean power.

**Open:** Whether it is later conquered, subordinated, joined by marriage, or federated.

### D-008 — There is no Habsburg Spain

**Status:** Canonical

The Habsburgs remain principally Austrian and Burgundian. Charles V never inherits the historical Spanish-American monarchy.

**Payoff:** France is less encircled; Europe becomes a shifting contest among France, Austria, the Western Crowns, England, Aragon, and German states.

### D-009 — The Reformation spreads before later containment

**Status:** Canonical

The Reformation begins near its historical date and may initially spread more easily without Charles V's composite monarchy. It is not already reduced to isolated pockets by 1526.

**Reasoning:** Later wars, failed alliances, destroyed patronage, repression, and exhaustion must cause its political containment.

## Exploration, colonization, and disease

### D-010 — Columbus is not funded in 1492

**Status:** Canonical

Portugal rejects Columbus, and no victorious court of Isabella funds him. Other powers do not immediately accept the complete proposal.

### D-011 — Northern exploration grows from Bristol

**Status:** Canonical framework; voyages provisional

English westward activity develops from Bristol's pre-Columbian merchant interest. Credible reports appear around 1498–1505; English mapping grows around 1505–1512; Breton and French vessels follow. By 1526 this produces fisheries, trade, and fragile stations rather than conquest.

### D-012 — Brazil is encountered around 1500

**Status:** Canonical

The India route and Atlantic wind system still carry a Cabral-like Portuguese expedition toward Brazil. Brazil becomes known, claimed, roughly mapped, and lightly exploited.

**Rejected:** Brazil remaining wholly unknown until the later colonial period.

### D-013 — Southern delay has several causes

**Status:** Canonical framework; incidents provisional

Delayed rival colonization of the Caribbean and lands farther south results from the interaction of:

- hurricanes and notorious expedition losses;
- disease, supply failure, and navigational uncertainty;
- Indigenous resistance;
- Portuguese charts, bases, convoy systems, and naval enforcement;
- treaty claims and legal intimidation;
- European wars and opportunity costs;
- the absence of a successful Caribbean launching platform.

**Rejected:** Hurricanes alone deterring exploration for 150 years.

### D-014 — Northern contact is multipolar

**Status:** Canonical

English, French, Dutch, Breton, and some German ventures compete in northern North America. Indigenous powers bargain among several weak entrants and trade access, information, and alliances.

### D-015 — Indigenous survival is institutional, not simple immunity

**Status:** Canonical

Epidemics remain catastrophic but are slower, regional, and staggered without rapid hemispheric conquest. Many councils, kin networks, food systems, ceremonial institutions, and military coalitions survive long enough to adapt and recover.

**Rejected:** Mild epidemics, rapid general biological immunity, or uniform outcomes across the Americas.

### D-016 — Technological equivalence is sector-specific

**Status:** Canonical guideline

American powers may equal European rivals in selected fields such as firearms, fortification, metalworking, printing, administration, medicine, sailing, or operational doctrine. They do not all follow one path or acquire identical industrial systems.

## The American New Reformation

### D-017 — The movement arrives in four waves

**Status:** Canonical framework; dates provisional

1. European refugees, printers, artisans, translators, and clergy cross the Atlantic.
2. Indigenous authors and institutions transform the imported traditions.
3. Revolutionary Covenant thought returns to Europe.
4. Post–Great Atlantic War renewal and anti-imperial movements reshape North America.

### D-018 — The New Reformation is Indigenous-authored

**Status:** Canonical

It is not European Protestantism with Indigenous decoration. Indigenous translators, jurists, spiritual leaders, diplomats, clan authorities, women, and printers become principal authors.

Possible resonances include accountable leadership, council government, reciprocal obligation, limits on alienable land, sanctuary, and layered sovereignty. These must be grounded in particular peoples rather than assigned generically to all Indigenous cultures.

### D-019 — “Covenant” is an umbrella

**Status:** Canonical

The Covenant tradition contains multiple languages, denominations, philosophies, and hostile factions. Conversion does not automatically mean cultural replacement; syncretism, strategic adoption, translation conflicts, and dual belonging remain common.

### D-020 — Sanctuary becomes a constitutional question

**Status:** Provisional but central

Indigenous states, maroon communities, northern ports, and dissident settlements shelter people escaping the later plantation system. Iberia calls this theft and interference with navigation; Covenant thinkers argue that unjust jurisdiction ends at a polity recognizing sanctuary.

**Payoff:** Caribbean wars, abolitionism, the Third Wave, and revolution.

## Slavery, empire, and revolution

### D-021 — The slave trade is later, not necessarily smaller

**Status:** Canonical framework

The absence of early Caribbean and mainland conquest delays plantation expansion. Once sugar, capital, shipping, and monopoly protection align in Brazil and the Caribbean, the trade can still become enormous.

**Rejected:** Treating delayed colonization as automatically humane.

### D-022 — Portugal becomes the principal southern colonizer

**Status:** Provisional

The Western Crowns expand plantation settlement in Brazil and later selected Caribbean islands. Other European powers remain concentrated in smaller northern ports and settlements.

### D-023 — Navigation Wars connect slavery with maritime law

**Status:** Provisional

Late seventeenth- and early eighteenth-century wars concern convoy inspection, neutral shipping, privateering, fugitive slaves, sanctuary, and Indigenous jurisdiction.

### D-024 — Enlightenment figures cannot be copied unchanged

**Status:** Canonical guideline

Locke, Montesquieu, Rousseau, and comparable people may exist, but their lives, patrons, works, and influence change. Their historical functions are distributed through a wider network of Indigenous, African, creole, Jewish, converso, refugee, and European writers.

### D-025 — Revolution begins in the Portuguese system

**Status:** Provisional

Debt, colonial warfare, slavery, sanctuary disputes, unequal representation, and Covenant thought combine in an eighteenth-century Portuguese imperial revolution.

A military ruler later plays part of Napoleon's structural role—defender and centralizer of revolution, conqueror, and source of exhaustion—but the identity remains open.

### D-026 — England's civil war is delayed and enlarged

**Status:** Provisional

A Stuart Settlement prevents the historical seventeenth-century Civil Wars. After the Portuguese Revolution, Third-Wave ideas and colonial agitators help ignite a longer, more destructive English conflict around 1790–1830.

### D-027 — An Atlantic Spring replaces a purely European 1848

**Status:** Provisional

Revolutions in the 1830s–1850s connect abolition, land, labor, federal representation, religious equality, and national self-rule across Europe and the Americas.

## Industrial warfare and the twentieth century

### D-028 — Iberian decline resembles an imperial “sick man”

**Status:** Provisional

The nineteenth-century Western Crowns retain possessions and privileges but become vulnerable to debt diplomacy, separatism, concession hunting, and foreign intervention. The Ottoman analogy is functional, not a copy.

### D-029 — American great powers develop imperial factions

**Status:** Provisional

Centralizing nationalists argue that industrial states need overseas bases, protected commerce, and African possessions. This conflicts with plural Covenant sovereignty.

### D-030 — A precursor war foreshadows global conflict

**Status:** Provisional

A South Atlantic War around 1890–1905, perhaps centered on Angola, allows Brazil or a Brazilian coalition to defeat Iberia. Like the Russo-Japanese War, it reveals changing power and industrial danger without teaching elites enough.

### D-031 — Naval capability outpaces land doctrine

**Status:** Provisional but important

Atlantic rivalry advances naval gunnery. Without the Danish, Austro-Prussian, and Franco-Prussian wars, European heavy artillery, mass-army administration, and land doctrine develop less coherently. Long-ignored artillery advocates are vindicated around 1922.

### D-032 — The Great Atlantic War begins as an old war

**Status:** Provisional

Early European operations evoke Blenheim, Leuthen, and Austerlitz. Mass rifles, machine guns, railways, entrenchment, and new heavy artillery destroy those assumptions.

A Nova Scotia fleet campaign combines Jutland-scale sea control with a Guadalcanal-like sequence of night actions, convoy battles, island fighting, and an Iberian marine landing attempt.

**Open:** Belligerents, joining order, outbreak, theaters, battles, and settlement.

### D-033 — North America's civil war misreads the previous war

**Status:** Provisional

Veterans overlearn artillery and fortification. Continental distances reward mechanized logistics, radio, air reconnaissance, armored columns, and deep maneuver.

### D-034 — The final Covenant campaign divides the National Union

**Status:** Provisional

The Pittsburgh/Forks corridor acts as a reversed Vicksburg; a march across Pennsylvania echoes Sherman's March; Adrian Kessel surrenders at Concord House in an Appomattox-like scene. A Covenant of North American Nations emerges alongside a smaller Laurentian nationalist remnant.

**Open:** Every proper name in this decision.

## Process

### D-035 — Review the timeline in fifty-year increments

**Status:** Canonical process

Each review covers chronology, differences from actual history, planted and paid-off threads, Iberia, Europe, the Americas, wider-world reverberations, and a critical plausibility audit.

Only **1476–1526** has completed this process. Later events are outline-level canon.

### D-036 — The repository is authoritative project memory

**Status:** Canonical process

Use the latest committed repository files instead of relying on conversational memory:

- `CANONICAL_TIMELINE.md` records what happens.
- `DECISIONS.md` records why and how firmly it has been decided.
- `AGENTS.md` defines the workflow for future Codex sessions.
